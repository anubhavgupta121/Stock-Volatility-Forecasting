Forecasting next-week S&P 500 volatility

Can machine learning beat GARCH, the standard model from finance? On unseen 2023–2026 data, yes: XGBoost trained on the QLIKE loss scored 20% better, and most of the gain came from one input, the VIX index.

Anubhav Gupta, roll no. 25110040November 2026Code and notebookTest forecasts (CSV)Validation forecasts (CSV)

In plain words

Prices on the stock market swing more in some weeks than in others, and banks and investors need to know how big next week's swings will be. This project trained computer models on more than 30 years of S&P 500 prices and tested them once on 2023–2026, years they had never seen. The best one scored 20% better than the standard finance model, mostly because it was given the VIX "fear index", but it still forecast too low when sudden shocks like April 2025 hit.

Abstract

This project asks whether machine-learning models forecast the next five days of S&P 500 volatility better than GARCH(1,1), the standard model from finance. Every choice was made on 2019–2022, and every model was scored once on 2023–2026. XGBoost trained directly on the QLIKE loss scored 0.308 against GARCH's 0.384, a 20% reduction that matches its validation edge (0.81× GARCH). Most of the gain came from one input, the VIX index: plain linear HAR given the same inputs (the range and VIX) came within 0.034, and a straight-line fit on VIX alone beat GARCH (0.3465 vs 0.3841). VIX helped most on nervous days, not in crashes, and the winning model has a hard ceiling of 4.2% a day, less than half the 2020 peak.

Test scores on 2023 to September 2026, lower is better (QLIKE is the primary metric, defined in §5.2). The full table with all nine models is in §6.2, and the explorer in §6.1 recalculates it for any date range.

Main findings

  1. Information beat model complexity. Linear HAR given the same inputs (the range and VIX) scored 0.43 on validation, close to the forest and XGBoost (0.416). VIX alone, fitted with a straight line, beat GARCH. §6.3
  2. Train on the metric you are judged by. Changing only XGBoost's loss moved it from HAR's side of the table to GARCH's. §4.6
  3. VIX helped on nervous days, not in the crash. Over February to May 2020 it made no difference. §6.4
  4. Tree models have a ceiling. The final model can never forecast above 4.2% a day. §7.3
  5. A forecast can look right and score worst. Naive hugs the actual line but is a week late at every turn. §4.1
  6. Plausible ideas failed on evidence. Smaller forest leaves, log-HAR and a GARCH-anchored hybrid were tested and rejected. §4

1The question

Can machine learning predict how much the market will move next week?

The question. Do machine-learning models (HAR regression, random forest, XGBoost) forecast next-week S&P 500 volatility better than classical baselines (naive, rolling average, EWMA, GARCH(1,1)) on unseen 2023–2026 data, including during volatile periods?

Why risk is predictable when prices are not. Every daily move is a direction times a size. Direction is close to a coin flip, but size is not: big moves follow big moves, and quiet days follow quiet days. A nervous market (March 2020) has open questions that take weeks to resolve and big moves that force more selling; a calm one (2017) has nothing forcing anyone to act. Volatility keeps only the size, so it keeps the predictable part.

What is forecast. Every trading day, one number: the typical size of a daily move over the next five trading days.1 A value of 0.010 means a typical daily move of 1%. Multiplying by √252 ≈ 15.9 gives the annual scale VIX uses, so 1% a day is about 16% a year.

Why it matters. Funds size positions with volatility forecasts, brokers set margins with them, and option prices depend on them. The project predicts risk only. It makes no claims about price direction or trading profits.

1Why a week, not a single day? One day's move is mostly luck, so it is a poor yardstick. Say the true volatility is 1% a day all week: on any day a typical move is about 1%. Real days scatter around that by luck, say 0.2%, 1.7%, 0.6%, 0.9% and 1.3%. A perfect 1% forecast then looks off by 0.8, 0.7, 0.4, 0.1 and 0.3 on single days, but combining the five days (square, average, square root) gives 1.08%, only 0.08 off. Worse, single-day scoring can rank a lazy forecast above a perfect one: over four days with true levels 1%, 1%, 2% and 2% a day and moves 0.3%, 1.6%, 1.4% and 1.2%, the perfect forecast's average error is 0.675, while a constant 1.5% scores 0.425.

What the data shows. Returns are centred on zero, with most days inside ±2%. Volatility clusters: thick bands in 2000–03, 2008–09 and 2020, quiet stretches in the mid-1990s and around 2017. The biggest up days sit inside crises, because direction flips but size stays big.

A data problem found and avoided. In Yahoo's S&P 500 data the opening price simply equals the previous close on 44–98% of days in 1990–2006, falling to about 0% from 2008. A feature built from the open (overnight gap, open-to-close) would be a fake zero for most training years and real in the test years, so open-based features were excluded.

3Preprocessing

Two inputs earned their place: the day's high–low range and VIX.

3.1The target

Log returns: the log of today's close over yesterday's. They add up across days (100 → 150 → 100 is +40.5% then −40.5% in logs, but +50% then −33% in percentages) and they are the standard in volatility models.

The target for day t is the volatility of days t+1 to t+5: square the five returns, average them, take the square root. The square removes the sign, so ups and downs don't cancel; the root brings the result back to percent per day.

target(t) = √( mean( r²t+1, …, r²t+5 ) )

Why not .std()? It subtracts the window's own average first, which hides steady trends. A week of +1% every day has a .std() of 0% but a volatility of 1%; a week of +2, +1, +3, +2, +2% gives 0.7% and 2.1%. The S&P's true average daily return is a few hundredths of a percent, so assuming zero costs almost nothing.

One row per day, overlapping targets. Neighbouring rows share 4 of their 5 target days. That keeps a forecast every day (about 9,200 rows instead of about 1,840 weekly ones), at the cost of rows that are not independent, which the evaluation has to respect.3

Five training days removed. On five days in 1990–2018 the S&P 500 closed at exactly the previous day's close, so today_vol = |r| is 0. Log-HAR (§4.3) takes the log of every input, and log(0) has no value, so these five rows were dropped from the training set (in the notebook's split cell). That is five rows out of about 7,300; the validation and test years were not touched.

3Checked by hand. The target on 1 February 1990 (0.007086) equals the past-5-day value on 8 February, as it should. In code: rolling(5) mean of r², square root, then shift(−5).

6Why HAC standard errors. The residuals come in long streaks (Durbin-Watson 0.44, healthy ≈ 2) because neighbouring targets overlap, so ordinary p-values are overconfident. Skew 1.85 and kurtosis 13.4 show a few huge crash-week misses.

4.3Log-HAR Rejected

Why it was tried. Least squares minimises squared errors in raw units, so crash weeks dominate the fit. The same 25% miss counts 56 times more in a crash week (0.06 predicted as 0.045) than in a calm one (0.008 as 0.006). Fitting HAR on logs makes every miss count by its ratio, as QLIKE does.

What happened. Converting back from logs runs low on average: outcomes 1%, 1% and 4% average 2%, but their logs average 0.462, and e0.462 is only 1.59%. QLIKE punishes too-low forecasts, so uncorrected log-HAR scored 0.668. The standard correction (multiply by eσ²/2) brought it to 0.561, which confirmed the bias but still lost to plain HAR (0.545). Plain HAR stayed.

4.4Random forest

500 trees on resampled training days, averaged. (Strictly, with scikit-learn's default of letting every split consider every feature, this is bagged trees rather than a random forest.) The first run (minimum leaf size 50) flattened at about 0.036 in March 2020 while the actual peaked at 0.090: a leaf of 50 days mixes a few crash days with many merely nervous ones.

A hypothesis that failed. Smaller leaves should let the forest reach higher in a crisis and improve QLIKE. Sweeping the leaf size from 5 to 200 showed the opposite: small leaves learned the noise of a few days and made every normal week erratic, while large leaves barely reacted. QLIKE traced a U-shape, with the best at leaf 65 (0.547). The crash is a few weeks out of about 1,000 validation days, so any crisis gain was outweighed by noise everywhere else.

4.5XGBoost

Trees are added one after another, each fixing the errors the earlier ones still make. With early stopping (scored on the last 15% of the training years), the number of trees times the learning rate came out the same at every rate: about 5.5 at depth 1 and 2.4 at depth 2. The data sets the total amount of correcting; the learning rate only decides how finely it is split into steps. Depth-1 trees won, with validation QLIKE 0.516.

Why the shallowest trees won. Boosting gets its strength from stacking many simple trees, unlike the forest, which needs each tree to be strong on its own. A depth-1 tree asks one question about one feature, so the model cannot combine features. That it still won suggests interactions between features add little here.

4.6XGBoost with a QLIKE loss

The mismatch. Plain XGBoost trains on squared error but is judged on QLIKE, and the two disagree about which misses matter. A calm day predicted at half its true volatility (0.0025 vs 0.005) scores QLIKE 1.61; a crash day 12% low (0.035 vs 0.040) scores 0.04. Squared error ranks the crash miss 4× worse. QLIKE ranks the calm miss about 40× worse.

The fix: train on QLIKE itself. QLIKE needs a positive prediction, so the model's raw output z is read as the log of the predicted variance, and a custom objective supplies the gradient and Hessian. The starting value is the log of the mean training variance, the constant that minimises the loss.7

L(z) = y·e−z + z − ln y − 1∂L/∂z = 1 − y·e−z∂²L/∂z² = y·e−z

y is the actual variance and ez the predicted variance.

Result: validation QLIKE 0.497, effectively tied with GARCH (0.494). Changing only the loss closed about 85% of the gap.

Depth mattered much more with this loss. The gradient 1 − y·e−z is large on spike days, where the actual variance is many times the forecast, so a few days pull hard. Deeper trees carved out small leaves for those spikes and overfit them (depths 3–5 scored 0.54–0.62); depth-1 trees cannot.

7Why not just train on the log of the target? Squared error on log y aims at the average of the logs, and e to that average sits below the real average. With variances 1, 1 and 16 the average is 6, but emean log is 2.5: a built-in under-prediction, exactly the miss QLIKE punishes most. Reading z as log variance keeps the loss comparing ez with y directly.

Each model wins on the metric closest to its training loss. Least squares (HAR, forest, plain XGBoost) minimises squared error, which is what RMSE measures. GARCH is fitted by maximum likelihood, which has the same structure as QLIKE. The QLIKE-trained XGBoost changed camps, with the same model and inputs and only a different loss.

4.7GARCH-anchored hybrid Rejected

Why it was tried. Trees can only output values built from their training leaves, while GARCH's formula has no upper limit. Giving GARCH to the trees as an ordinary feature would not remove that cap, so instead each prediction started from GARCH's own forecast: XGBoost's per-row starting point (base_margin) was set to log(GARCH variance), and the trees learned a multiplier on GARCH. A leaf value of −0.2 multiplies GARCH's variance by e−0.2 = 0.82 (its volatility by 0.90). The cap then applies to the multiplier, not the level. GARCH's coefficients were fitted on data before the cutoff only, then used to forecast every day, so the training rows got GARCH values without seeing the future.

The hybrid against plain XGBoost-QLIKE on the same inputs, by validation year. Shading compares each cell with GARCH in the same column: blue is better, red is worse, and stronger shading means a bigger gap.

The hybrid beat GARCH in every year, but plain XGBoost-QLIKE on the same inputs did too, and it scored better overall (0.458 vs 0.467) and in the 2020 crash (0.695 vs 0.724). So the anchor never paid off, and with VIX the gap grew (0.400 vs 0.435). Anchoring to GARCH drags forecasts towards GARCH's own errors, its late peak and slow decay: in April 2020, after the peak, GARCH scored 0.43 against 0.10 for XGBoost-QLIKE.

5Evaluation design

Tune on validation, test once.

5.1The split

The split is by date and never shuffled. Each part contains a stress episode, so models are fitted, tuned and tested on both calm and turbulent markets. Features and targets are computed on the full series first, then sliced, so no period loses rows to warm-up windows.8

Two things to know about the test run. Two planned checks were skipped before freezing the setup: the trees without the range feature, and a depth recheck on the final features. And the split leaves no gap between periods, so the last 5 training rows of each fit have targets that use the first days of the next period. That is a small data leak: 5 rows out of about 8,000. A check added after the test run dropped those rows and refitted: validation scores moved by at most 0.0003 and test scores by at most 0.0003, so the overlap changes nothing.

Is the edge tied to one split? As a second check, each validation year was forecast by models refitted on all data before it (walk-forward, with the same 5-row purge). XGBoost with the QLIKE loss beat GARCH in every year, by 12–28%, and it was the only model that did: linear HAR + range + VIX lost to GARCH in 2020 and 2022, and the forest and squared-error XGBoost lost in 2022.

Walk-forward QLIKE on the validation years: each year scored by models fitted only on earlier years. Shading compares each cell with GARCH in the same row; the best score in each row is in bold.

8Why the test set is used only once. If ten people each flip a coin 20 times, the one with 14 heads is lucky, not skilled. Five equally good models also score slightly differently by luck. Picking the best one on the test set and reporting its score overstates it, because it was chosen partly for its luck. Any choice made by looking at test results turns the test set into a second validation set.

5.2Metrics

QLIKE (primary). With v the actual variance and h the predicted variance, each day scores

QLIKE = v/h − ln(v/h) − 1

averaged over days. It depends only on the ratio, so a calm week and a crash week count equally, and it punishes under-predicting risk more than over-predicting: with actual variance 1, predicting 0.5 scores 0.307, predicting 2 scores 0.193.9

RMSE (secondary): the typical error in volatility units. It weighs over- and under-prediction equally and is dominated by crash weeks.

Relative to GARCH: scores only mean something in comparison, and calm years score lower for every model, so results are also given as a ratio to GARCH, the strongest baseline on validation.

9Why QLIKE works on variance, not volatility. A noisy estimate is right on average for variance, but not for its square root. If true variance is 1 and two weeks' estimates come out 0.5 and 1.5, they average 1.0, but their square roots (0.707 and 1.225) average 0.966. QLIKE's proven property, that it ranks forecasts correctly even against a noisy target (Patton), relies on working in variance.

HAR caught up to 0.43, against 0.416 for the forest and plain XGBoost, all three trained on squared error. Most of the improvement over GARCH came from the information in VIX, and the QLIKE loss added about 0.02 on top. The range helped HAR on its own (0.545 → 0.529) but added almost nothing once VIX was in (0.4275 → 0.4265): for a linear model the two carry largely the same signal.

VIX on its own. A straight line fitted from VIX to the target, with no other input, scores 0.435 on validation and 0.3465 on test, beating GARCH on both (0.494 and 0.384). Raw VIX, used as it is, scores 0.507 and 0.425 and loses to GARCH: VIX tends to run above the volatility that follows, and the fitted line scales it down. So a single, well-scaled number already beats the standard finance model. XGBoost with the QLIKE loss still beats that line on test, by 0.038 (95% interval 0.011 to 0.075).

6.4Before and after VIX

VIX was the largest single gain on validation (0.458 → 0.400). The chart shows the same model, XGBoost with the QLIKE loss, trained without and with VIX, next to GARCH. Switch versions to see what changed, and pick a period to see where.

In the crash, VIX barely moved the ceiling. Both versions flat-lined from 12 March for about three weeks, at 3.55% a day without VIX and 3.79% with it, while actual volatility ran at 3–8%. GARCH reached 6.0% on 16 March, five days after the actual peak, and was still about 4% in early April, when actual volatility had fallen to about 2%. The flat top is the tree ceiling measured in §7.3: a depth-1 tree makes one cut, say "range above 0.03", so its top group mixes a few crash days with many merely nervous ones. The inputs were not the main problem: high_low_1 peaked at 0.051 in 2020, inside its training range (0.066), and the forecast was still capped.

A small lift still counts under QLIKE. Against the 9.0% peak, a forecast of 3.55% scores 3.55 and one of 3.79% scores 2.90, because QLIKE punishes under-prediction steeply. That is why VIX helped in March. It gave the gain back in mid-February, when VIX was low and the VIX version forecast lowest of all just before the crash.

Where VIX did help: nervous days. Almost half of its gain came on the quarter of days with actual volatility between 1.2% and 2%. VIX works in both directions. After the December 2018 sell-off it told the model within days that the market had calmed, while past-return inputs kept the other forecasts high for weeks. In summer 2020 it kept the forecast up while realised volatility fell, so the model was ready for the September sell-off.10

10Why 2022 scores lowest of all. The market fell about 25%, but volatility stayed steadily high without sudden jumps. QLIKE measures surprise relative to the forecast, not the level of risk.

6.5The April 2025 tariff shock

Choose “Apr 2025” in the explorer (§6.1). Actual next-week volatility jumped from about 1.2% to 5.4% a day. All models reacted about a week late. That is expected: the target is next week's volatility, so the actual line rises on 27 March, before the 2 April tariff announcement nobody could have known about.

XGBoost peaked at about 3.7% and linear HAR + range + VIX, which has no ceiling, at about 3.6%. So missing information limited both, not the trees. XGBoost won 2025 (0.481 vs GARCH 0.601) on the way down: with VIX it fell back in mid-April, while GARCH decayed slowly and stayed too high into May.

7.3A hard ceiling on the forecast

A depth-1 boosted model's forecast is a sum of one leaf from each tree, so there is a highest value it can ever give. Feeding the final model a row with every feature at 10× its training maximum gives that ceiling: 0.042, against a training maximum target of 0.090. A shock the size of March 2020 would have been capped at less than half its peak. In 2025 the cap was not reached (peak forecast 0.037), but only 0.005 of room was left.

Why so low, with 2008 and 2020 in training? Boosting adds trees, but for a group of days that share leaves the sum stops growing once their gradients cancel, which with the QLIKE loss happens at the mean variance of those days. For example, 10 crash days at 0.09 and 140 after-crash days at 0.035 settle at about 0.041: the crash days push up, the calmer days push down, and they balance.

8Limitations

  • The winning model has a ceiling. It can never forecast above 0.042. GARCH and linear models can keep climbing in a record crash.
  • One market, one test period. Results may not hold for single stocks, other indices, or a regime unlike anything in 1990–2026.
  • The target is an estimate. Five daily returns give a noisy measure of volatility; intraday data would be more precise but was not freely available for the full history.
  • A small leak at the split edges. The last 5 training rows before 2019 and before 2023 have targets built from the first days of the next period. Removing them moved every score by at most 0.0003 (§5.1), so the results stand.
  • Few crises to learn from, and overlapping targets mean fewer independent observations than rows (HAR's Durbin-Watson statistic is 0.44).
  • Backward-looking inputs lag unscheduled shocks. No model here anticipated 2 April 2025.
  • Small margins between ML models. A block bootstrap puts the 0.014 gap to the forest at −0.003 to 0.033, so it may be luck (§6.2).
  • Final fit hit the tree limit. On validation (fit on 1990–2018), early stopping chose 771 trees. On the 1990–2022 refit it never triggered (1,998 of 2,000 trees), so the test model is not exactly the validated one. It was not rerun, because the test set had been used. A labelled check afterwards capped the refit at the validated 772 trees: it scores 0.310 (0.81× GARCH), so the conclusion does not depend on it.
  • GARCH(1,1) treats rises and falls alike, although falls tend to raise volatility more; richer GARCH variants were out of scope.
  • Forecasting volatility is not a trading strategy. Features predict volatility without causing it, and the reasons given for differences between models are explanations backed by tests, not proofs.

9Conclusion

ML beat GARCH, mostly thanks to better information.

On unseen 2023–2026 data, every ML model given the intraday range and VIX beat every classical baseline on QLIKE and RMSE. XGBoost trained on the QLIKE loss was best (0.308 vs GARCH 0.384), and its edge matched validation (0.80× vs 0.81× GARCH).

The bigger lessons are about how forecasts win. The information mattered more than the model: linear HAR with the same inputs came within 0.034. And VIX earned its place in ordinary nervous weeks, not in the crash itself. The loss mattered next: each model wins on the metric it is trained for, and training XGBoost on QLIKE moved it from HAR's level to GARCH's and then ahead. And every model has a shape to its mistakes: naive is a week late, GARCH decays slowly, and boosted trees cap out at 0.042, which is the main reason not to trust the winner blindly in the next record crash.
